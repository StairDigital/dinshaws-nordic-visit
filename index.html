Hygiene

  • Chilled, not aseptic; can sit behind HTST, ESL or UHT processing.
  • Described as "ultra-high hygiene", between a basic pasteurised filler and an aseptic one.
  • Shelf life is usually limited by processing and distribution temperature, not by the filler.
  • Hot-climate references: Japan, Korea, Indonesia and Malaysia.

Flexibility

  • 2 sides: 1 product split across both, or 2 products from 2 processing lines.
  • One side can be cleaned while the other runs.
  • 4 pack families: Mini, MiniV, Midi and Base, from 200 to 1,000 ml.
  • Same volume, different impression: a slim 250 ml MiniV reads premium, a squat 250 ml Mini reads everyday.

Packaging cost

  • Cost index: standard 100, plastic barrier 125, aluminium foil 130.
  • In Europe, packaging is about 4% of a yoghurt retailing at about EUR 2.
  • Chilled: 30 to 120 days without foil. Ambient: 4 to 9 months with foil.

What the session showed

How yoghurt markets evolve

The global path

  • Plain yoghurt, then plain drinkable, then creamier drinkable, then spoonable.
  • India runs the other way: set dahi first, with lassi alongside.
  • Latin America: drinkable is huge and leads innovation.
  • USA: Greek yoghurt drove a boom over 10 to 15 years.

Ambient in China

  • About 50 Tetra Top machines with Yili and Mengniu, about 1.5 bn packs.
  • Heat-treated after fermentation, stable at pH 4.2 to 4.4 in a foil-lined pack.
  • Built on a cultural preference for room-temperature drinks and costly inland cold chains.
  • Ambient yoghurt with inclusions sells above 40 RMB.

Protein and taste

  • Valio, Lindahls and Danone lead with 25 to 26 g protein per pack.
  • Scandinavian products are deliberately less sweet; the Dinshaw's team found the sweetness right.
  • Pear and vanilla, a local favourite, was a flavour not seen in India.
  • The protein case is stronger in India, where vegetarian diets leave a protein gap.

Agreed next steps

  • Dinshaw's to send measurable requirements: products, raw-milk bacterial load, target shelf life, pack format and inclusion sizes.
  • Tetra Pak to propose the full line, from processing to distribution.
  • Test viscosity and shelf life at the Product Development Centre, including a 60-day run at 10 to 14°C.
  • A dedicated market intelligence session on yoghurt and fermented trends, with costing support up to full concept development.

Quality

What makes or breaks a yoghurt

  • Sensory: texture and viscosity, mouthfeel, taste.
  • Shelf-life limits: yeast and moulds, post-acidification, separation and syneresis.
  • Levers for both: formulation, process design and operation, and choice of culture.

The line diagram that followed carried the headline "Every step can impact the quality", with each station on it marked for its effect.

Concentration

Minimise raw material cost and acid whey

For high-protein products such as skyr and Greek-style yoghurt, the yoghurt is concentrated after fermentation. 2 routes were compared for making 1,000 kg of 10% protein product per hour from 3.5% protein milk.

Nozzle separator
Feed 3,031 kg/h
Whey 2,031 kg/h · 0.3% protein
Ultrafilter
Feed 2,857 kg/h
Whey 1,857 kg/h · 0% protein

Same output from both. The ultrafilter route needs 174 kg/h less milk and produces 174 kg/h less acid whey, because no protein is lost in its whey.

Market direction

Products on the rise

  • Spoonable and drinkable high-protein products.
  • Ambient yoghurt.
  • Products for specific target groups, such as people on GLP-1 diets, and products with creatine.

Chapter 5

The Dinner Table

Over dinner the conversation moved from the plant to the market: where the white space sits in Indian dairy, what makes a line pay, and where ice cream and cheese are heading.

  1. Drinkable is the gapLassi is the mass-market entry route, with drinkable yoghurt as a premium layer once consumers are ready.
  2. UHT pays at volumeA UHT line needs about 17 hours a day of production as a bare minimum.
  3. Naming drives adoptionProbiotic curd, high-protein skyr and lassi are clearer labels than "yoghurt".
  4. Shelf-life sweet spot of 40 to 60 daysDesign for 60, declare 40, so the retailer gets about 30 days on shelf.
  5. Repeatability is the next milestoneThe drinkable test batch succeeded once, which proves the product is achievable.
  6. Cheese is structural growthCheddar is expected to double by 2030, and cheese also absorbs surplus flush-season milk.
  7. Ice cream will differentiate on formatExtrusion shapes and stickless bars are hard to copy; inclusions are not.

The UHT line

  • Runs flavoured milk, milk, high-protein milk, drinking yoghurt and buttermilk.
  • About 6,000 litres go to drain at each start before the line stabilises.
  • Heat can denature protein and create a burnt note; the first sip decides acceptance.
  • Parameters are fixed at design; changing them later means recirculation and about 3 hours of CIP.

Drinkable yoghurt

  • About 50% of yoghurt in Europe is drunk, against about 15% set or stirred.
  • Lassi sells at about Rs 25 per 200 ml; a drinkable at about Rs 50 needs clear added value.
  • Raw milk of good quality is a prerequisite, and Dinshaw's has it.
  • Target consumer: high protein, low fat.

Shelf life and process

  • A 3 to 4 month high-protein product needs sterilisation at about 105°C or more, which affects taste.
  • Extended-life lassi at 40 to 60 days opens Mumbai and other markets.
  • Skyr is not UHT: separation or UF, to be recommended by Tetra Pak.
  • Agreed sequence: fix consistency, taste and flavour first, then select the process.

Cheese and surplus milk

  • Powder and cheese are the 2 outlets for flush-season milk, each with about 1 year of shelf life.
  • A cheese plant needs about 3 lakh litres a day and 30 to 40 days of ripening.
  • B2B cheese buyers book 3 to 4 months ahead.

Ice cream formats

  • Stickless bars and mini bites grew from about 4 to 5% to about 7 to 8% of the market in 4 to 5 years.
  • India's paper take-home tubs are expected to move to IML.
  • About 95% of volume still comes from regular products.

Paneer technology

  • Only 3 continuous lines existed globally, from Obram: 2 at Amul and 1 in Canada.
  • Tetra Pak has spent 2 to 3 years combining continuous curd making with block forming.
  • The technology was adapted from European cottage cheese.
Chapter 6

The Night Session

A late-evening working session in which Zervin Rana walked through Dinshaw's draft product definitions, shelf-life targets, fruit needs and process flows for the new yoghurt plant. This is the requirements list Tetra Pak asked for.

Shelf-life goal

A 45-day product, designed for 60 days at 4°C.

Within Dinshaw's it will be held under 4°C. After it leaves the plant, 10 to 12°C may be the temperature, so it should last 15 days after leaving the plant.

Product slate

  • Set curd / dahi
  • Greek yoghurt: protein 7 to 8%, fat under 2%
  • Skyr: protein 10 to 12%, fat under 1%
  • Lassi
  • Drinkable yoghurt

Open questions

  • Fruit pieces of 8 to 10 mm: berries, nuts, seeds, strawberry.
  • How will dry fruit be handled? How will fruit prep be handled?
  • Raw milk bacterial load, to be provided by Verma sir, decides between Bactofuge and microfiltration.

Dahi

Standardised milk · F 3.5% · P 4.5% · RO
  1. Bactofuge, homogenise, pasteurise
  2. Chill to 45°C
  3. Add culture
  4. Fill in cups
  5. Incubate in the cup
  6. Blast freeze
  7. Finished goods store at 4°C

Greek Yoghurt

Standardised milk · P 8% · RO / UF
  1. Bactofuge, homogenise, pasteurise
  2. Chill to 45°C
  3. Add culture and ferment
  4. Break curd
  5. Strain whey by filtration or nozzle separator
  6. Whisk; add sugar and fruit paste
  7. Thermise
  8. Fill in cups, fruit via PCM dosing

Skyr

Skimmed milk · F 0.5%
  1. Bactofuge and pasteurise
  2. Add culture and rennet
  3. Ferment
  4. Strain whey mechanically by filtration or nozzle separation
  5. Whisk drained curd until smooth and creamy
  6. Add sugar and fruit paste
  7. Thermise
  8. Fill cups, fruit dice added

Lassi

Standardised milk · recipe-wise
  1. Bactofuge, homogenise, pasteurise
  2. Chill to 45°C
  3. Add culture and ferment
  4. Break curd and add sugar
  5. Thermise
  6. Add flavour
  7. Fill
Also from the night session
  • Regulatory headroom: Codex requires at least 2.7% protein for yoghurt and 5.6% for a concentrated fermented milk, so the Greek yoghurt and skyr targets clear the bar comfortably.
  • Live-culture claim: the draft flows thermise Greek yoghurt, skyr and lassi, so a "live" or probiotic claim would need cultures added after heat treatment.
  • Fruit and texture: pieces up to 8 mm pair with thinner yoghurt and up to 10 mm with thicker yoghurt, so fruit size and texture are specified together.
  • Reading future consumers: AI can project how a child aged 5 in 2026 will eat in 2040; the view is sharper for a state than for a country, but each level shows the direction, which is towards health.

The heritage corridor

Early equipment and vending machines are kept on display beside the history wall.

Entering the clean zone

Visitors follow the site's HSE and hygiene rules before entering production.

  • Visitors wear a white coat, white shoe covers and red headgear that encloses all hair.
  • Hands are washed thoroughly before entering the clean zone.
  • Jewellery, watches and visible piercings are not allowed in production.
  • Visitors keep a minimum distance of 2 metres from open product.
  • Peanut-containing foods are not allowed in the building.
  • Children under 7 years are not allowed in the factory.
